# Does Fallibilism Have a Moral Gravity?

## Idea

Popperian fallibilism — and by extension the Clarity Process, which is fallibilism applied to interpersonal understanding — may have a directional pull on the ethics of its practitioners. Specifically: people who internalize "we know harm more reliably than we know flourishing" tend to converge on negative utilitarianism (minimize suffering) rather than positive utilitarianism (maximize happiness) or rule-based deontology.

Popper himself made this move explicitly in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (footnote in vol. 1): we should aim to minimize avoidable suffering rather than maximize happiness, because the epistemic grip on what is bad is firmer than the grip on what is good. "Piecemeal social engineering" is negative utilitarianism as political method. This is not a statistical drift — it is a doctrinal pipeline embedded in the founding text.

The deeper claim worth testing: **the asymmetry between argument-driven and evidence-driven moral flips.** Deontology-to-utilitarianism flips happen through sustained questioning ("why is that rule load-bearing?") — thought-experiment pressure erodes rule-intuitions faster than it erodes consequence-intuitions. Utilitarianism-to-deontology flips almost never happen through argument; they happen through **failure events** — Stalin for the Cold War generation, FTX/longtermism collapses for EA, naive QALY-maximization producing absurdities. Rules return as epistemic humility against motivated reasoning, not as discovered moral truth.

If this asymmetry is real, the Clarity Process inherits it. A community that institutionalizes Popperian verification will, statistically, drift suffering-focused — and will only correct back toward rule-bound constraints after a visible failure of its own.

## Open Questions

- Is the spectrum framing (deontology ↔ utilitarianism) even the right axis? Modern metaethics treats consequence/rule/character as more orthogonal. The article may need to recast the question in axes terms before answering it.
- Empirical: do we have any longitudinal data on moral-framework "flips"? PhilPapers Survey gives distributions but not flow. Worth a footnote on the absence.
- Is there a third pathway — character/virtue ethics — that absorbs fallibilist practitioners differently than NU does? Possibly the route taken by clinicians, therapists, and others whose work is suffering-adjacent but who resist the calculus framing.

Two fallibilist-obligation refinements:
- **Bartley dissolves the tu quoque.** Pancritical rationalism (Bartley) answers the "but you can't criticize your own criticism" regress, removing the standard objection that fallibilism is self-undermining — worth using to clear the ground before asking whether fallibilism has *moral* gravity. Pairs with Deutsch's optimism applied to a solubility framework.
- **The asymmetric trust rule** (a derived obligation, not a vibe): discount a person's *conclusions* by their calibration, but **never** discount a *criticism* by it. A poorly-calibrated source can still land a valid refutation; rejecting the criticism because of the source is the genetic fallacy wearing epistemic-hygiene clothes.
- **Commitment ≠ self-deception.** "Never commit to outcomes you can't control" is too strong: public commitment to an uncontrollable outcome can be causally productive (precommitment, mobilization). The fallibilist sin is confusing the *bet* for *control* — not making the commitment. Wisdom is staying honest about epistemic status while choosing external language for its causal effect.
