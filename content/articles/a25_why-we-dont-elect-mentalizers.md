# Why We Don't Elect Mentalizers

## Idea (and the political risk to manage)

The recursive-understanding argument is usually framed at the dyad: two co-founders, two partners. This article extends it to **hierarchies** — what happens when a population elects a leader who is *incapable* (not merely unwilling) of recursive understanding across differences.

## The wolf-rabbit objection, and why it collapses at scale

The standard pushback: "in pure conflict of interest — wolf wants to eat rabbit — recursive understanding doesn't help. So what if the rabbit knows the wolf knows?"

The objection holds only in the **sterilized two-actor, asymmetric-power** case. It collapses for populations:

1. **Retaliation capability changes the game.** Many wolves, many rabbits, both able to strike back = mixed-motive, with mutual destruction reachable. This is Schelling's entire domain (*The Strategy of Conflict*, 1960): under mutual vulnerability, accurate modeling of the opponent's reasoning is the difference between deterrence and catastrophe — not a nicety.
2. **The Cuban Missile Crisis is the canonical case.** Kennedy forced "how does this look to Khrushchev?" Jervis (*Perception and Misperception in International Politics*, 1976) documents recursive-understanding capacity as the thing that averted lose-lose. The faction that refused to model the Soviet view favored escalation.
3. **Aumann's agreement theorem** gives the formal hook: two rationals with common knowledge of each other's posteriors cannot agree to disagree. A leader who *blocks* common-knowledge formation preserves disagreement that need not exist.

## The leader as common-knowledge generator

A national leader doesn't only manage their own ambiguity (Pinker's dyadic frame). They shape what their *population* takes as common knowledge of red lines, intentions, and costs. A leader who can't model the adversary also can't generate accurate common knowledge for their own side. The dyadic blindspot in Pinker's account is exactly the gap this article fills.

## The uncomfortable core — selection effects

Even if mentalizing capacity in leaders improves collective outcomes, **democratic selection may not reward it.** It may reward charisma, in-group signaling, or grievance-channeling — which can be *anti-correlated* with cross-group mentalizing. Two open questions:
- Can voters *detect* mentalizing capacity from campaign behavior? (Largely unstudied.)
- Does the selection mechanism reward it even if they can?
