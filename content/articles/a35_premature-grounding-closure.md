## 1. Core thesis (positioning spine)

Cognitive psychology has the *need for cognitive closure* (Kruglanski & Webster): the drive to seize an answer and freeze on it. Psycholinguistics has the *grounding criterion* (Clark): the point where two people mutually believe the listener understood "well enough for current purposes" — and stop checking. The first governs closure on *validity*; the second, closure on *meaning*. The same seize-and-freeze dynamic runs on both, but the meaning case is worse for two reasons. First, the referent that would correct a validity error is the world, which pushes back; the referent that would correct a meaning error is only the other mind, and it arrives late, lossy, and after the cost is paid. Second, people don't even run their grounding criterion on accuracy — felt understanding tracks warmth and similarity, not comprehension — so the criterion is routinely met falsely and silently. Call this *premature grounding closure*. Its consequence: it sits *upstream* of debiasing, because you cannot correct someone's bias through a channel whose meaning never grounded. Verified comprehension is therefore not a debiasing technique but the precondition for one.

## 2. The key distinction (validity-closure vs meaning-closure)

Validity-closure is self-correctable in principle: the world is a shared referent that pushes back. Meaning-closure has only a *lagged and lossy* referent — downstream behavior eventually reveals the mismatch, but late, partially, and after the coordination has already failed, by which point hindsight bias has rewritten both parties' memory of what was agreed. So premature meaning-closure is far more weakly self-correcting than validity-closure, and effectively self-concealing in real time.

*(Note: do not write "no referent" or "the only referent is the other mind." That overclaims and is the first thing a reviewer pulls. The defensible claim is "lagged, lossy, post-hoc referent.")*

## 3. Conceptual lineage and placement

The underlying object — recursive mutual belief — was formulated independently across fields, which is itself worth noting (convergence as a sign the object is real):

- Lewis (1969): "common knowledge"
- Schiffer (1972): "mutual knowledge*"
- Aumann (1976, "Agreeing to Disagree"): formal/game-theoretic common knowledge
- Stalnaker (1973/1978/2002): "common ground"
- Higgins/Echterhoff: "shared reality"; Pickering & Garrod (2004): "interactive alignment"

Clark & Marshall (1981) identified the **processing paradox**: humans can't compute the infinite recursion, so they short-circuit it with heuristics (e.g., physical co-presence). The *grounding criterion* itself is Clark's (Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs 1986; Clark & Schaefer 1989; Clark & Brennan 1991; Clark 1996).

**Placement of the Min Principle:** a tractable replacement for the uncomputable recursion — a bounded paired estimate (joint comprehension capped by the lower self-assessment), descended from Pinker but solving the Clark–Marshall problem. This is the precise, citable slot. *(Flagged for inclusion.)*

## 5. Empirical backing already in hand

**People substitute feeling for accuracy.** The sharper part of the thesis has solid empirical support. *(Flagged for inclusion.)* Felt understanding / perceived partner responsiveness is driven substantially by warmth, emotional similarity, projection, and motivated reconstrual rather than by actual comprehension:

- Feeling understood can predict relationship quality more strongly than the partner's *actual* responsiveness (Gordon & Diamond 2023 review).
- Greater emotional similarity → more *perceived* responsiveness (Sels et al. 2020).
- Perceived responsiveness reflects motivated reconstrual, not just enacted responsiveness (Lemay & Neal 2014).

**The relational substrate already moves bias.** Perceived partner responsiveness enhances intellectual humility and weakens self-serving biases — better-than-average effect, overclaiming, hindsight bias (Reis et al. 2018). This is ammunition for the upstream-of-debiasing argument, with one caveat: it shows *felt* responsiveness affects bias directly; the dossier's distinct claim is that *verified* grounding gates debiasing *uptake*.

## 6. Upstream-of-debiasing argument

Interpersonal debiasing ("you have bias X," then discussion) presupposes that the message about the bias was grounded. If grounding fails, the instruction is mis-received and cannot work. Strongest for *communicated* debiasing; weaker for *self-administered procedural* debiasing (a checklist), though even that was learned via an understood instruction once. Since most real-world debiasing in teams and coaching is interpersonal, the precondition bites where it counts.

This replaces the earlier "benchmark to prove separateness" framing. The right experiment is an **interaction**: debiasing-alone vs grounding-then-debiasing.

Sharpens the 2026-06-15 Aumann paragraph above from "black-boxes meaning-verification" to a located diagnosis, and supplies the reply to the objection that paragraph currently invites.

- **The failure sits at the interpretation layer, not the priors layer — and that is a different diagnosis from every standard one.** The standard explanations for why Aumann fails in practice are *differing priors* or *communication cost* (Aaronson's route: the hypothetical conversation might take absurdly long, so complexity explains persistent disagreement). Neither asks **whether the signal was received as sent.** Common knowledge is defined over events; in real dialogue the event each party conditions on is *their private interpretation of an utterance*. So the theorem's own explicit precondition — common knowledge of the other's belief — is never established. Note the strength ordering this implies and a65 confirms: Aumann is a **stronger** case than Popper, because common knowledge is a stated antecedent of the theorem rather than a buried presupposition.
- **The formalist reply, and the answer.** Expect: *"interpretation differences are just information-structure differences, already in the model."* Answer: the formalism can absorb it, but absorbing it makes the theorem **practically vacuous** — if partitions are private and unverified, common knowledge of posteriors is unattainable in principle and the theorem describes a state no conversation reaches. That is a stronger conclusion than the complexity route, because it says the barrier is not bandwidth, it is **the absence of an error-check**.
- **Cheap novel demonstration, currently unbuilt — but "does convergence fail?" is the wrong question.** Two agents, shared priors, message passing through an unverified interpretation layer. The bare failure result proves nothing: if the interpretation layer is unverified, common knowledge does not obtain *by definition*, so the theorem is silent rather than falsified — a simulation cannot fail to reproduce a tautology. The version that earns the day is **parametric and differential**. Parametric: sweep the interpretation-error rate ε and ask *how* convergence degrades — graceful degradation weakens the claim (small verification failures cost little, and dialogue tolerates them); a threshold or phase change is the finding. Differential: bandwidth (Aaronson) and differing priors both predict *slow or absent* convergence, so only a run where agents converge **confidently on a shared wrong belief, with no alarm firing**, separates the interpretation-layer account from its two rivals. **Corrected 2026-08-07 — do not spec this.** The signature is not the payload, because confident-wrong consensus is already established in at least three unrelated mechanisms: information cascades (Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch 1992 — information-value collapse, not decode error), DeGroot with a single stubborn agent, and evolutionary signalling models that name "uninformed consensus on a wrong belief" as a stable phase. The ε-sweep is also answered, and answered against the graceful-degradation framing: noisy opinion dynamics show a hard phase transition, not smooth decline. What is *not* clearly done is the **route** — reaching confident-wrong consensus from misread meaning between agents who share priors — but that is a much narrower and more fragile claim than a novel signature, and two 2026 multi-agent-LLM papers (arXiv 2603.04474 error cascades + governance layer, ~89% catch rate; an ICML 2026 biased-consensus phase-transition paper) may already cover it. **All of the above is abstract-level only — no full paper was read. 1. **Unverifiability of common knowledge is textbook — it cannot be the finding.** The literature states plainly that verifying common knowledge requires checking infinitely many conditions, and that mutual knowledge to any high *finite* order is insufficient (Bonanno & Nehring survey; Aumann 1976 p.1236). ### Article material — the worked example to carry (Lederman's Evens & Odds)

Use this in the draft. It does the argumentative work that prose cannot: it shows the theorem breaking while every condition Aumann *names* is satisfied, which is the exact move this article makes one layer over.

Aumann's key conditional, quoted verbatim (1976, p. 1236): *"if two people have the same priors and their posteriors for an event A are common knowledge, then these posteriors are equal."*

Lederman's first countermodel (§3.1, Figure 1). Six possible states, 1–6; the actual state is 1. **Evens** distinguishes the world only in pairs — she can tell {1,2} from {3,4} from {5,6}, never within a pair. **Odds** distinguishes it in triples — {1,2,3} from {4,5,6}. Both are perfect Bayesians conditionalizing on their own evidence. Ask both for the probability of E = "the state is 2, 4 or 6":

- Evens answers **1/2**. Odds answers **1/3**.
- **They do share a prior** at the actual state — uniform, 1/6 each.
- **Their disagreement is genuinely common knowledge.** Evens answers 1/2 at *every* state and Odds answers 1/3 at *every* state, so each proposition is the whole state space — self-evident to everyone, known to all orders. Aumann's stated antecedent holds.
- **And they still disagree.**

The leak is one rung up the ladder: they share the prior but **do not commonly know that they share it.** Odds knows they both hold the uniform prior, but cannot rule out state 3 — and at state 3, Evens would consider it possible that Odds holds a different prior. So Odds does not know that Evens knows they agree.

**The rhetorical use.** Lederman's four culprits (common knowledge of *which* prior; of conditionalization; the KK principle; updating only on true propositions) are all assumptions Aumann *used in the proof* but never wrote into the sentence. This article claims one more unstated assumption of the same kind — **that both parties took the same meaning from what was said.** Lederman supplies the precedent and the template for the move; he never looks at language, which is where the remaining room is. Cite him as an ally, and note his result is the *stronger* one: he breaks the conditional with shared priors and common knowledge of posteriors both intact.

**Precision the draft must hold: the claim is about meaning, not words.** Two parties can agree on every word — same vocabulary, same sentence, no ambiguity either would report — and still condition on different events, because the event is what the words *meant to each of them*. This is why "common language" is a mask rather than a solution (a18), and why the failure is invisible from inside the conversation: nothing at the word level is wrong. Any phrasing that says "decoded the words differently" understates it and invites the objection that careful wording would fix it. It would not.

**Hard ceiling that must travel with any Clarity-Flip claim (Halpern & Moses, coordinated attack):** over any channel with nonzero failure probability, common knowledge is unattainable no matter how many acknowledgements are exchanged — each ACK moves the uncertainty up one level and never removes it. So verification **reduces but cannot eliminate** the failure mode. Stating otherwise is provably false. This also independently confirms the point that kills the binary simulation: unverified channel ⇒ no common knowledge, by definition, no simulation required.
