# "I Got It. We Don't Need to Waste Time on This."

You've just explained something important. Not small talk — something that matters. A decision, a plan, a concern you've been carrying.

You want to make sure you're aligned. So you ask a simple question: "Can you play back what you heard?"

And the wall goes up.

"I got it. We don't need to waste time on this."

Or worse: "Do you think I'm stupid?"

You weren't questioning their intelligence. You were trying to close a gap you've learned to feel — the gap between what you said and what they understood. You've been burned enough times to know that "I got it" means nothing without verification.

But now you're the problem. You're "wasting time." You're "overthinking." You're the difficult one.

---

If you know this feeling, this post is for you. Not because you need to learn about listening — you already practice it. You paraphrase. You check. You ask "what do you mean by that?" You do the work.

And you're exhausted. Because most people around you don't.

They nod. They say "makes sense." They move on. And three weeks later, when the misalignment surfaces — when the project is wrong, when the plan falls apart, when the relationship ruptures — they're genuinely surprised. They thought they understood.

**They didn't know they didn't understand.** That's the blindspot. And you can see it, but they can't.

---

There's a name for what you're feeling. Cognitive science calls it the [Illusion of Transparency](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1998-01347-006) — the belief that our thoughts are more obvious to others than they actually are. When someone speaks, they hear the full symphony in their head. The listener hears a few notes and fills in the rest from their own experience.

In a [classic experiment](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1998-01347-006), researchers asked people to tap out the rhythm of a well-known song. Tappers predicted 50% of listeners would recognize it. **The actual rate was 2.5%.** An overestimation by a factor of twenty.

That's every conversation where someone says "I was clear" and the other person walks away with a different understanding.

There's a related bias called the [Curse of Knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge) — once you know something, you literally cannot imagine not knowing it. So the speaker assumes their meaning landed. The listener assumes their interpretation is correct. Both feel confident. Neither verifies. Both are wrong.

A [2023 meta-analysis of 400,000 observations](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-023-09897-5) confirmed what you already know intuitively: listening drives real outcomes. Sales performance (r=.47), reduced burnout (r=.28), better work relationships (r=.51). **Everyone agrees listening matters.**

But here's the finding that should make you angry: **75% of all listening research relies on self-reports.** People rating their own listening ability. Which is exactly the miscalibration you see every day — the person who's worst at listening is most confident they're great at it.

---

So why does this keep happening? Why do smart, capable people resist the simple act of playing back what they heard?

Three structural reasons — what I call the [Three Asymmetries](https://claritypledge.com/manifesto#the-three-asymmetries-that-make-verification-hard):

**1. Role Asymmetry.** In any conversation, someone needs to be the listener. Actually listening — not waiting to talk. But we naturally default to speaker mode. When both people are in speaker mode, nobody verifies anything. The conversation becomes [two parallel monologues](https://claritypledge.com/manifesto#the-three-asymmetries-that-make-verification-hard). If neither person can reproduce what the other just said, no information crossed the gap.

**2. Information Asymmetry.** Only the speaker has access to their own intent. The listener has words plus their interpretation. That interpretation *feels* like understanding — but it's a guess. [Research from Harvard and UCLA](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/the-listening-gap-speakers-assume-theyre-heard-the-spoken-to-often-feign-attentiveness) found that listeners' minds wander 24% of the time, and speakers can't detect it. The listener's "I understood" is not evidence of understanding. Only the speaker can verify.

**3. Vulnerability Asymmetry.** When the listener reacts — a sigh, an eye-roll, a defensive "yeah yeah I got it" — the speaker shuts down. Truth stops flowing. The person asking for verification gets punished for asking. So they stop asking. That's what happened in every conversation where you backed down because pushing for clarity would "damage the relationship."

These aren't personality flaws. They're structural features of how conversation works. Knowing them doesn't make you immune — but it gives you language for what you've always felt.

---

You're not overthinking. You're not difficult. You're not wasting time.

You're the person in the room who knows that "I got it" is not evidence of understanding. You know this because you've paid the price when it wasn't true — in projects, in partnerships, in relationships.

The question isn't whether verification matters. You already know it does. The question is: **why doesn't anyone measure it?**

That's what the next post is about.
