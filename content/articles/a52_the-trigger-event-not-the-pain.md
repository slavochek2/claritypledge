# The Trigger Event, Not the Pain

**Arc:** ARC-4 (The Assumption Nobody Questioned) — the premise under nearly every founder's segment choice is "biggest pain wins." It doesn't.
**Source conversation:** "Overcoming impostor syndrome in sales" (2026-07-14) — strategic layer only; the personal material stays out.

## Idea

Every piece of segment advice tells you to find the deepest pain. So you find it, you build for it, and then you can't sell it — and you conclude something is wrong with you.

The premise is wrong. **Pain doesn't buy. A dated trigger event with a budget owner buys.**

Score segments on six dimensions and the ranking inverts:

| Dimension | Why it's weighted |
|---|---|
| **Dated trigger event** | double — is there a specific date something changed? |
| **Non-personal budget** | double — does the money come from a line item, not a wallet? |
| **Quantifiable failure cost** | double — can they name the number in 30 seconds? |
| Felt-now pain | is it hurting today, or is it a deferred good? |
| Evidence base | do you have proof this works here? |
| Reachability | can you get to them at all? |

The uncomfortable result in our own case: the segment with **the deepest evidence base** — 30+ sessions, years of work — scores **worst** on exactly the three double-weighted axes. No dated trigger. No budget owner. No number anyone names unprompted. The segment with the thinnest evidence base wins decisively on all three, and loses only on the dimension that is cheapest to fix.

## The reframe that makes this worth reading

There's a thing that feels like impostor syndrome when you go to sell: the doubt that shows up right at the moment of asking. The reflexive reading is that it's a confidence defect.

The alternative reading — and the one worth testing — is that **the doubt is a correctly-firing model.** If your evidence sits in a segment with no trigger and no budget, then "will they buy this" is not insecurity. It's your own buyer-model reporting a true negative. The fix isn't confidence. It's a different segment — and you're not building a new product, you're rewrapping.

The honest counter, which the article must carry: *"my avoidance is actually integrity"* is exactly the kind of self-concealing story this framework warns about. It is the flattering version. So make it testable rather than picking it:

- **The armchair diagnostic:** don't ask "do I feel avoidant" — that's armchair about armchair. Count dated facts: hours theorizing vs hours in buyer contact over four weeks. The tell isn't the volume of thinking; it's the **timing** — a new framework arriving precisely when an empirical test became available.
- **The flaw rule:** *a flaw is a legitimate gate only if a buyer could detect it in the first session.* A spotted flaw is a conjecture, not data, and doesn't get veto power over the contact that would test it. Pre-commit to log-and-go; after five contacts, check how many logged flaws a buyer actually raised.

## The flaw this segment actually has (and the article shouldn't hide)

The winning segment has a named, session-one-detectable problem: **power asymmetry breaks paraphrase in an employment context.** A new hire has every incentive to perform comprehension and score generously. The Min Principle assumes two honest self-estimates; an employment relationship doesn't obviously supply two.

That is a real threat to the thing the scoring model just selected — and a piece that presents the scoring model without it is selling the reader a method that fails on the author's own case.

## Why article-worthy

- It inverts advice the reader has certainly heard ("find the biggest pain") using a scoring model they can run in ten minutes.
- The "your doubt is your buyer-model, not your character" reframe is genuinely useful and not self-help.
- It's honest about the winner's fatal-looking flaw, which is what makes the method credible rather than a pitch.
- The kill criterion is concrete: **if 5 target founders can't name a mismatch cost within 30 seconds, the quantifiability score was fantasy and the ranking collapses.** Knowable in a week.

## Open questions

- Does the flaw rule (buyer-detectable-in-session-one) generalise, or is it a founder-specific rationalisation guard?
- Is "evidence base" genuinely the cheapest dimension to fix, or is that the convenient conclusion?
- Can the Min Principle survive an employment dyad at all? If not, the scoring model chose a segment the instrument can't serve — and the model needs a seventh dimension: *does the instrument work here?*
