# Sanitation, Not Eradication

**Arc:** ARC-2 (The Invisible Made Visible) — misunderstanding-harm is the thing nobody measures; the Clarity Letter is the instrument; the institutional-accounting gap is what it reveals. (Secondary: ARC-4 — the accepted premise that "the cure just needs to be better" hides the real dependency.)
**Source conversation:** "Illusion of shared understanding as a cognitive bias" (2026-07-21), ~9 rounds of pressure-testing.

## Idea

Start with the tempting frame: the illusion of shared understanding is a civilizational malady — upstream of broken deals, broken relationships, bad politics — and, like malaria, it has high burden × cheap correction × neglect, so eradicating it would be unusually high-leverage.

The frame survives contact only after two corrections, and the corrections are the article.

**Correction 1 — eradication is the wrong verb.** These biases (curse of knowledge, illusion of transparency, closeness-communication bias) aren't pathogens you remove. They're the price of efficient cognition — the curse of knowledge is the flip side of expertise. You can't eradicate them without eradicating the compression that makes thinking work. The honest public-health analogy isn't malaria eradication; it's **sanitation**. Snow and the Broad Street pump: you don't eliminate cholera's conditions, you build sewers and clean water so the harm becomes rare. That maps onto coordination infrastructure and it makes a *falsifiable* promise instead of a utopian one.

**Correction 2 — "root cause" overclaims, and the scoping criteria already know it.** A large share of broken deals come from perfectly-understood, genuinely-opposed interests — verified understanding sometimes *sharpens* conflict rather than dissolving it. So misunderstanding isn't the root cause of "all of it." But the three segmentation criteria — fuzzy intent, compounding duration, concentrated consequences — already carve out exactly the zone where misunderstanding dominates over raw interest-conflict. The framework scopes the claim correctly; the malaria/root-cause rhetoric un-scopes it. Trust the criteria over the slogan.

## The turn — why the cure never spread

The understanding "vaccine" already exists in a dozen forms — NVC, Crucial Conversations, motivational interviewing, double-crux, pre-mortems, deliberative democracy. None became default infrastructure. **This is not a supply problem. It's a demand-legibility problem.** You don't buy a vaccine for a disease you can't perceive you have, and this failure is self-concealing — the illusion erases its own evidence. Cholera killed at scale until Snow's map made the invisible mechanism *visible*. The bottleneck was never clean water; it was legibility.

Which is why the **Clarity Letter is doing more strategic work than the session**. The letter is the Snow map. It makes the invisible burden legible, and legibility is the precondition for wanting the treatment. Measurement manufactures the demand every prior initiative lacked.

> **Honesty beat (keep this in the article, don't sand it off):** "legibility is the bottleneck" is asserted, not proven. Competing explanations survive — cost (the cure is genuinely expensive), incentive (strategic ambiguity is often useful), status (asking "did I get you?" reads as low-status), partial-diffusion ("I-statements" already spread). The Clarity Letter is the experiment that runs the fork: it raises visibility without lowering the cure's cost, so if people read it, nod, and still don't show up, the constraint was cost/incentive, not legibility. The venture is the test of the thesis — better than being right by assertion.

## The institutional escalation — the accounting layer

Move the claim from individuals to institutions and it gets stronger, not weaker. Aviation CRM and surgical checklists became *mandatory* — but only after crashes and deaths made the burden countable in bodies. Institutions optimize what they can measure and attribute. Misunderstanding-harm is diffuse, deniable, unattributable: a deal dies and everyone blames strategy, not the comprehension gap three meetings back. No metric, no owner, no budget line.

Upgraded thesis: it's not that individuals can't *see* it — it's that institutions can't **account for** it, so it never competes for strategic priority against things that show up on a balance sheet. That reframes what ClarityPledge might be — not a better communication practice (there are dozens) but an **accounting layer** that makes comprehension-risk attributable, so it can finally compete for institutional priority.

> **Counter to hold on stage:** maybe institutions don't account for it because it genuinely matters less at scale than raw interest-conflict — the absence could be correct triage, not a blind spot. If the ROI were there, wouldn't some competitive org have stumbled into pricing it and won? The move is to find the one domain where the corpses are countable enough to prove it. Aviation had crashes. What's ours?

## Why it's article-worthy

Costly-signal credibility: the piece publishes its own hedges (the legibility thesis is unproven; the root-cause claim overreaches) while making a sharp, memorable case (sanitation not eradication; the letter as Snow map; the accounting layer). It's the ARC-2 shape done at civilizational scale, and it seeds the strategic reframe — CP as attribution infrastructure — that the strategy docs are only now beginning to register as an untested bet.

## Enrichment (2026-07-27) — the honesty beat has a sharper edge than "unproven"

The honesty beat above says the legibility thesis is *asserted, not proven*. There is a stronger version, and it should replace the softer one in the draft:

**The thesis in its current form predicts its own absence of confirming testimony.** Self-concealment says comprehension failure hides itself — people misattribute it to personality, priorities, execution. So "nobody names it as their problem" is *exactly* what the framework expects. Which means that observation cannot distinguish between **the thesis is right and invisible** and **the thesis is wrong**. A claim that predicts the absence of its own evidence is unfalsifiable as held — and I'd be merciless about that if someone else brought it to me.

What that kills is narrower than it first looks: it kills **selling the diagnosis**, not the thesis. Nobody buys "you have an illusion of understanding." They buy relief from things they already complain about out loud — rebuilt the same feature two or three times; a decision that keeps getting relitigated; a signed-off spec that shipped wrong; conversations that end in agreement and then diverge. Misunderstanding is my *explanation*; those are their *symptoms*. I'd been selling the explanation.

That belongs in this article because it is the same move the piece already makes about Snow: the bottleneck is legibility, and the first thing that needs to become legible is whether my own evidence says what I think it says.
