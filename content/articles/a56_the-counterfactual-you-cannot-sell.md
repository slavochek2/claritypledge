# The Counterfactual You Can't Sell — Why Prevention Goods Get Bought Without Proof

> Working title — not final. `[FOUNDER DECISION: title]`. Alternatives: "Falsifiable in the Aggregate, Unattributable in the Particular" · "A Blood Panel Doesn't Prove It Extended Your Life."

## The idea

Spend long enough trying to justify a preventive product and you arrive at a wall: you cannot show the buyer the disaster that didn't happen. The instinct is to read this as a flaw in your idea. It isn't — it's a **category property**. Insurance, security audits, preventive medicine, prenups, legal review: none of them can prove a counterfactual, and every one of them gets sold anyway.

The article's sharp move is a distinction that dissolves the paralysis: the claim *"verified comprehension reduces coordination failure"* is **perfectly falsifiable at population level** — run cohorts with and without the protocol, measure splits, abandoned builds, failed hires, over time; clear refutation conditions. What is unavailable is **attribution at the unit of the individual sale**. You can't run one company twice. So the claim is falsifiable in the aggregate and unattributable in the particular — and the sales conversation happens in the particular.

Two consequences follow, and both are counterintuitive:

1. **You don't need a better argument. You need a frame that doesn't require attribution.** Prevention gets bought through five known routes, none of them proof: recent vivid loss (emotional attribution), professional norm ("serious teams do this"), mandate (regulation, investor, board), bundling to something with a legible output, and price below the threshold where anyone audits. Naming the routes converts "how do I prove ROI" into "which route am I on."
2. **Diagnostics don't need counterfactuals at all.** A blood panel doesn't prove it extended your life; it tells you a number you didn't have. Showing someone a measured comprehension gap on a decision they are making *this week* is immediate, legible value with no downstream attribution required. The ROI question dissolves because you stop claiming an outcome and start selling information. That also gives a selection criterion for which decision types to enter: **attributability at the point of sale**.

And a third, which is the piece's best ending: if evidence is generatable at population level, it isn't out of reach — just out of reach for a single transaction. Which means **every free instance is a data point no paid session could produce.** Free distribution and evidence generation turn out to be the same activity.

## Why it's compelling (ARC-4)

The assumption nobody questions is "if you can't prove ROI, you don't have a business." The hidden dependency is *attributability at the unit of sale* — a property no prevention good has ever had. The reader arrives holding a demand they'd never make of their own insurer.

## The counter-thesis to hold — this article argues against a54

a54 ("Sanitation, Not Eradication") lands on the **accounting-layer** thesis: institutions can't attribute misunderstanding-harm, so CP could become the layer that makes it countable. This article puts pressure on exactly that ambition: no other prevention good has an accounting layer, and they all sell. Either the accounting layer is a genuine unfair advantage *or* it's an attempt to solve a problem the category has always routed around. **Do not resolve the tension silently — name it and let both stand.** That disagreement is the most interesting thing either piece contains. Cross-link both ways.
