# A Pledge Is Not a Norm — What Bicchieri Says About the Artifact I Named the Company After

> Working title — not final. `[FOUNDER DECISION: title]`. Alternatives: "Normative Expectations Are Free" · "The Part That Works With Cardstock."

## The idea

A pledge is a **normative expectation**: a statement that people ought to do something. Bicchieri's finding is that normative expectations alone do not produce conformity. A social norm requires *both* normative expectations (others believe I ought to) and **empirical expectations** (I can see that others actually do). Missing the empirical half, a pledge is a promise everyone endorses and nobody enacts — structurally identical to "we communicate well," a stated preference at zero cost.

That is an uncomfortable finding for a project called ClarityPledge, and the article's value is that it's told by the person holding the bag.

The taxonomy that makes it precise, and which the article should teach cleanly:

- *Verified mutual comprehension* — the **good**. The thing itself.
- *Not pretending to understand* — a **moral norm**. Roughly unconditional; installs without coordination.
- *Revealing your gaps* — a genuinely **social norm**. Costly to the individual, beneficial to the group. Classic collective-action shape, and it needs the full apparatus.
- *Quantifying gaps* — a **practice/instrument**, not a norm. (The Min Principle is a measurement protocol.)
- *Predictable ways to bridge gaps* — an **institution**. Ostrom's rules-in-use. Predictability is what makes a norm followable at all.

The strategic consequence: treating all of these as needing the same pledge-and-event machinery spends coordination capital on the ones that don't require it.

**Where the empirical expectations actually come from** is the article's payoff. Not the pledge — the *room*. Visible acceptance at signup, visible practice during the event, and participants rewarding each other when someone does the hard thing. That is the machinery that converts a normative expectation into an empirical one. Pluralistic ignorance breaks when people *see* others doing it, not when they agree it would be good. Which reorders the whole architecture: **events are not distribution for the pledge — the pledge is downstream of the events.**

And the cheapest possible finding: this mechanic tests with **paper name tags**. No platform, no build. If you build software to test the norm mechanic, you've spent months on the one part that works with cardstock.

**The risk that keeps it honest:** a visible badge is a status signal, and status signals get adopted without the underlying practice. That is faking understanding one level up — the exact failure the instrument exists to detect, reappearing in the instrument's own adoption layer. Whether the badge tracks the practice or replaces it is an empirical question the first offline room answers.

## Why it's compelling (ARC-4)

The assumption nobody questions is that commitment devices change behavior. The hidden dependency is that a commitment is only half a norm, and the missing half is *visibility of others*. Costly-signal credibility: the author is auditing his own namesake artifact and reporting that it doesn't work the way the name implies.

## The scoped claim — do not overreach

Gap-revelation ranks in three different hierarchies and the article must keep them apart:

1. **Substantive importance: low.** It loses nearly everywhere it competes — against face-preservation (admitting confusion threatens your face *and* implies the speaker explained badly: a double face-threat), efficiency norms, deference to hierarchy, group solidarity. In most rooms it reads as incompetence, obstruction, or disloyalty. Empirically a dominated norm.
2. **Procedural priority: highest.** You cannot correctly apply honesty, fairness, loyalty, or falsification to something you've misconstrued.
3. **Activation priority: conditional** — and this is where the design work lives.

Norm conflicts don't get resolved by ranking; they get resolved by which norm is *focal*. Three mechanisms: **domain partitioning** (a bounded space where the ordering inverts — courts, confession, Chatham House, retrospectives; Clarity Live is one), **re-categorization** ("this isn't a pitch, it's a calibration"), and **cost-shifting** (sanction the concealer, not the discloser, and the ranking flips without anyone arguing about values).

The bound to state plainly: **a norm that fires everywhere isn't a norm, it's a tax.** Universal gap-revelation is an infinite regress that grinds coordination to zero. Outside its activation conditions — fuzzy intent, delayed feedback, concentrated stakes — concealment is *correct*, because verification cost exceeds misunderstanding cost. Publish the scoped version; the strong version ("comprehension norms should be high-status") hands any critic an obvious case where verification is the wrong move, and the whole ladder comes down with it.
