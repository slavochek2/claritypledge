# The Design That Made Me Load-Bearing — A Growth Plan That Contradicted Its Own Product

> Working title — not final. `[FOUNDER DECISION: title]`. Alternatives: "A Protocol That Rewards Performing the Protocol" · "The Scale Plan That Scaled Me."

## The idea

I drafted a growth design — a gamified, AI-certified field organization with tiers, quests, peer sign-off, and a data loop — and then ran it against four questions drawn from my own framework. It failed all four, and the fourth failure was fatal in a way that generalizes far past this draft: **the mechanism contradicted the content.**

The four questions, which are the transferable part:

1. Does it lower the *participant's* cost — time, attention, and the social exposure of admitting they might not understand?
2. Can one person run it, once, without me?
3. Does it separate the free artifact from the revenue activity?
4. Does the mechanism contradict the content?

Three ways the draft contradicted itself, each worth its own paragraph:

- **MLM-style recruitment architecture.** The draft said "without financial exploitation" — but the distrust MLM generates isn't only financial, it's the recruitment-for-status mechanic itself. Of every product in the world, a comprehension-verification protocol is the one that cannot smell like manipulation.
- **A certification gate.** The draft correctly identified NVC's certification loop as a limiter on spread — then designed a certification system and fixed its *speed*. But the constraint was never speed; it was the gate. Liberating Structures spread precisely because there is no authority to pass through.
- **Field quests.** "Catch someone asking a basic question and validate them" makes the other person a target rather than a participant. The Min Principle is *joint* comprehension bounded by both parties' honest estimates — you cannot run it on someone unilaterally. And scoring epistemic quality for progression invites optimizing the score: **a protocol for detecting performed understanding, redesigned to reward performing understanding-verification.**

Then the inversion that names the piece: the design most focused on scale — tiers, reviews, a repository, two markets — was the one that made *me* most load-bearing. It described an organization I would have to run.

## The reviewer was also wrong — and that's the second half

The four-question framework, applied honestly, encoded **one** diffusion mechanism as though it were the only one:

- **Cheap and open** (Liberating Structures, EventStorming): adoption because cost falls below the threshold where anyone deliberates. Wide, fast, shallow, weak retention, no revenue hook.
- **Scarce and selective** (fellowships, guilds, elite programs): adoption because membership confers standing. Narrow, slow, deep, strong retention, natural revenue hook.

Question 1 was written for the first and applied to a design built on the second. Both mechanisms are real, and the second is arguably better suited to a protocol that takes effort to do properly. The article should carry both halves — the draft was wrong, *and* the critique that killed it was narrower than it presented itself. That double correction is what makes it ARC-1 rather than a takedown.

## What survived

The problem statement, which is the sharpest one-sentence version yet written: **asking "I don't understand" carries a social cost, so people fake it.** No jargon, and most people recognize it immediately.

And the sequencing lesson: Braver Angels does have volunteer tiers and moderator training — but that structure arrived *after* the workshop format demonstrably worked, not before. Sequence, not absence.

## The version that survives the four questions

One named move, one page, no gate, no recruitment, no recording, done with someone who agreed to do it. Note also the practical wall the draft ignored: uploading audio of real conversations requires the other party's consent, which in the EU is not friction, it's law.

## Why it's compelling (ARC-1)

A design is stated in full, each link is tested against criteria the author already published, weak links are exposed, and the tightened version is offered with the vulnerability named — including the vulnerability in the testing criteria themselves. The reader watches rigor happen, twice.

## Second independent instance of the MLM-shape hazard (2026-08-10)

The recruitment-architecture point above was reached by testing a *draft* against the author's own criteria. It has since been reached again, from the opposite direction — by asking what the intended audience would see.

The structure under discussion was not the guild draft but the live plan: pledgers open clarity organizations, recruit members, members pledge, leads sign partner agreements with other leads, and conversion plus onward-conversion rates get measured. Read from outside, that is **a franchise with a conversion funnel** — and the rationalist / AI-safety community, which the recognition path in `docs/theory-of-change.md` depends on reaching, has an unusually strong immune response to that shape.

Two things this adds to the article:

1. **Intent does not travel.** The guild critique turned on the mechanic contradicting the content. This one turns on something weaker and harder to fix: the mechanic *resembling* something else. A norm cascade and a downline are structurally similar from the outside, and the reader's cheaper reading wins.
2. **Two mitigations, both stateable in public.** The protocol must be **usable with zero affiliation** — no org membership required to run a session — and **R₀ gets published honestly, including failures.** Any structure where standing inside the network depends on recruiting will read as the thing it resembles, regardless of what it is.

This strengthens the piece rather than repeating it: the same hazard found twice, by two different methods, once by self-audit and once by audience simulation.
