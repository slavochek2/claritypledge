# The Stopping Rule Nobody Specified

> Working title — `[FOUNDER DECISION: title]`. Subtitle candidate, and the sharper statement of the object: *"when to accept divergence with a specific person as final."* Alternatives: "Five Failures Are Not Evidence" · "The Deutsch Gap."

## Arc

ARC-4 (The Assumption Nobody Questioned) — a widely accepted premise (problems are soluble given the right knowledge) turns out to presuppose an operation its author never specified (how a bounded agent decides to stop). Trace the consequences, propose the fix.

## The object — say it this way, not the other way

**Not** "when to abandon a research programme." That framing invites an easy objection and puts the piece in Lakatos's territory, where the general problem is known to be unsolved.

**Yes:** *when to accept divergence with a specific person as final* — a stopping rule over a **social** process, with a counterparty who can be queried. Narrower, cleaner, and it is the version that has a tractability argument (below).

## Move 1 — The lede: five failures

Open with this. It is the most visual thing in the source material and it carries the whole argument.

> You argue with a colleague five times about a hire. Nothing moves. You conclude: we'll never agree.
>
> What supports that conclusion? Five failures.
>
> But each failure has two possible causes — your reasoning didn't land, or it landed and they still disagree. You never checked which. So the five failures support the pessimistic conclusion no better than zero failures do. They are consistent with both stories. They *feel* like accumulating evidence and they are not.

Then the turn: after verification, a failure means something specific — *this reason arrived intact and did not move them.* That is one real observation. **Verification does not make "hopeless" testable. It makes your failures count as evidence rather than noise.** People are stacking up non-evidence and calling it experience.

## Move 2 — The statement of the gap

Deutsch's optimism principle asserts that problems are soluble given the right knowledge, and that we cannot predict which problems will be solved when. Those two commitments together leave every actual agent — finite time, finite attention — with no principled basis for deciding when to keep investing in a problem and when to stop. Every agent nonetheless has a stopping rule. Deutsch's framework does not supply one, and taken literally implies you should never stop.

**Three supports for an absence claim** (it is harder to establish than a presence claim, and the draft must say so):

1. Deutsch himself separates solubility-in-principle from prophecy. Guaranteed eventual solubility plus unknowable timing yields no allocation guidance — the gap follows from his own commitments, not from a misreading.
2. Stopping rules are empirically real and demonstrably unreliable. Nelson & Leonesio's "labor-in-vain effect": judgments about how learnable something is drive time allocation, and people terminate study before mastery *even when instructed to master every item with unlimited time available.* A rare case where a stopping rule was measured rather than asserted.
3. **Lakatos is the strongest card.** The progressive/degenerating distinction exists *precisely because* falsificationism supplied no rule for when to abandon a programme, and Lakatos explicitly denied "instant rationality" — the criterion is retrospective. The tradition **recognized the stopping problem and declined to solve it prospectively.** That is much stronger than an absence argument: the gap is documented, not merely unfilled.

## Move 3 — The counter you must answer

A knowledgeable critic will say the stopping rule *is* there: criticism. Continue while your conjectures survive criticism and generate new problems; shift when they don't.

The reply: criticism-as-stopping-rule presupposes you can tell whether the criticism was **understood**. Otherwise "they rejected my argument" is confounded in exactly the way the lede describes. That folds this gap into the Popper Gap and makes them one claim with two faces — which is why this article is second in the series and opens by using the first.

## Move 4 — Conversation has a Duhem-Quine problem

This is the connection worth building the piece on.

Popper's problem was Duhem-Quine: a theory never faces evidence alone, only bundled with auxiliaries, so a failed test never tells you *which* element to abandon. Falsification therefore never *mandates* giving up.

Conversation has its own version. When your argument is rejected, the observation is bundled — either the argument is wrong, or it wasn't understood. **"They didn't understand me" is the auxiliary hypothesis that protects the core, and it is available indefinitely and free of charge.** Everyone uses it. Verification is what strips it away. Verified comprehension is the interpersonal analogue of specifying which auxiliary you are holding fixed — the move that makes a social test capable of degenerating.

## Move 5 — Why this one is tractable where Lakatos's wasn't

Lakatos showed a general *prospective* criterion isn't available **for theory choice**. This problem has one feature his lacks:

> **A theory cannot tell you whether you understood it. A person can.**

There is a verification signal here that theory-appraisal does not have. So the negative result does not transfer, and that asymmetry is the argument for tractability. One paragraph — do not oversell it.

## Move 6 — The social stopping rule

Four properties, and one structural observation:

- **Exit is the only unilateral move.** Continuing requires coordination; quitting requires nobody's agreement. The cheapest action available to anyone at any moment is the one that ends the process for everyone.
- **Announcing it is expensive.** "I've stopped trying to resolve this with you" reads as a verdict on the person, not the problem — so it goes unspoken, and the group never gets a moment where it could decide to invest more.
- **The others operate on a stale model.** They believe the process is live; their moves are addressed to a participant who left. This is where "we keep discussing it and nothing happens" comes from — you are not in a discussion, you are in the residue of one.
- **Re-entry has no protocol.** Coming back requires admitting you'd left, so people stay out.

The facilitation consequence is small and concrete: ask, out loud, *"are you still trying?"* It is the only way a silent exit becomes visible while the room is still assembled.

**Deferred, do not claim in this article:** the conjecture that a group's effective persistence is bounded by the *lowest individual* persistence — a second Min result in a different domain. Founder call: this needs separate checking and gets its own spec. If it is claimed here before it is checked, the article inherits an unverified structural claim it does not need.

## Move 7 — What "stuck" actually means

Definition earned across the piece: **stuck = confounded disagreement plus no procedure to de-confound it.** You cannot tell whether more effort would help, so effort stops. That is hopelessness, defined without reference to stakes or duration.

Plain-language version for anyone who needs it without the jargon: *there are two different things going on and you can't tell them apart from where you're standing.* Either they haven't grasped what you're saying, or they've grasped it perfectly and still disagree. Both look identical from the outside — same argument, same wall.

Prior art to cite, not to claim: Gottman's **gridlock** (entrenched positions, hidden agendas beneath the stated issue, and — importantly — an endpoint of *disengagement*, not escalation: the loudest gridlock is silent). Schweinsberg, Thau & Pillutla (2022) split impasses into **wanted** (both parties benefit from no agreement), **forced** (one party does), and **unwanted** (neither does) — only the third is in scope for a comprehension instrument.

## Move 8 — Bar-Tal, and the claim to actually make

Bar-Tal documents that perceived irresolvability is causal and self-sustaining, but treats it mostly as an *effect* — of prolonged violence, zero-sum framing, collective memory. He does not ask whether the belief is epistemically justified.

The addition: it often is. If comprehension was never verified, repeated failure to persuade carries no information about solubility, so the pessimism is a rational inference from a confounded signal — not merely a psychological adaptation.

**Two guards the draft must carry.**

*Do not overreach on scope.* Bar-Tal's cases are violent intergroup conflicts driven substantially by material stakes and leadership incentives. Claiming intractability is "really" a verification failure is a serious overreach and anyone who knows the literature will catch it. The defensible claim: verification failure is *one* generator of perceived irresolvability, and it is the cheapest one to remove, so remove it first as a control.

*State the status honestly.* That "one generator" claim is **a conjecture with a coherent mechanism, indirect support, and no direct test.** Do not write it as established. Kelman's interactive problem-solving is the nearest precedent — forty years on the hardest available case, with comprehension work producing something where "irreconcilable values" was the default explanation. Read its limitation as ours too: in-room change is real and does not transfer by itself. That is this project's distribution problem, already documented at scale.

## Move 9 — Two claims the source conversation got wrong, corrected here

Both were stated to the founder and both need repairing before they reach a draft:

1. **"Verification tells you whether to look inside the room or outside it."** Does not survive as stated. You can only verify propositions someone actually uttered; failure of the uttered set does not establish that nothing else in the room would work. Repaired: **verification converts an unbounded search into a bounded one** — a finite set of propositions confirmed transmitted-and-received, each markable tried/failed, plus a named remainder. You haven't shrunk the world; you've made part of it enumerable.
2. **"It makes pessimism falsifiable."** Wrong, and wrong in an interesting way. "This is hopeless" is a negative existential — already falsifiable, by one success. What it cannot get is *corroboration*, because failures are cheap and always attributable to something else. The correct claim is the Move 1 formulation: verification changes whether failed attempts **corroborate**. Use that; it is more Popperian and it is true.

## Falsification criteria — in-article, not in a footnote

**What would count against the thesis:** settings with **high verification and undiminished hopelessness**. Skilled mediation with formal restatement protocols is where to look. If many such cases exist, the factor is small. *Go look before publishing* — this has not been done.

**The testable version, small enough to run:** in groups reporting stuck, measure predicted-vs-actual paraphrase accuracy and perceived resolvability, before and after. If accuracy is high going in, the generator is not operating there.

## Note on the grounding claim

The founder's line — grounding has to happen interpersonally first, by steelmanning the strongest counterarguments, and only then in the world — is close to Habermas's core position: validity claims are redeemed discursively, understanding is the precondition for any claim reaching testable status. **He is already in this lineage.** The novel part is not the thesis, it is the operationalization: Habermas gave the ideal speech situation, famously untestable; this offers a measurable substitute. Frame it that way or a philosopher hands you Habermas in the comments.
