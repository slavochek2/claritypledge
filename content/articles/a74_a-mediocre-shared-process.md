# A Mediocre Shared Process Beats an Excellent Private One

> Working title — `[FOUNDER DECISION: title]`. Alternatives: "Six People, Six Feedback Protocols" · "Why I Offered It to You After You Said No."

## Arc

**ARC-2 (The Invisible Made Visible)** — a cost that reads as a personality clash is shown to be a protocol mismatch, then priced at three scales until it can't be ignored.

## Idea

Two people give feedback by different private rules. One states the flaw directly and means it as respect. The other expects context first, and reads directness as contempt. Neither is wrong about feedback; they are running different protocols. So the content never gets evaluated. The form does.

**Why sameness matters more than quality:** if everyone knows feedback arrives in one shape (*here's what I understood, here's my concern, here's what I'd need*), then hearing that shape tells you nothing about your standing. The process absorbs the threat so the content can be examined. Without a shared shape, every piece of feedback is also a message about the relationship, and people optimise for the relationship signal instead of the truth.

## The three scales (the source conversation priced each one)

- **Pair.** An owner corrects a young employee bluntly. The employee learns that pointing upward is expensive, and months later mentions a failing machine as "it's probably fine, maybe it's just me." The owner hears uncertainty, not a warning. The machine seizes. *(Source figure: roughly six weeks of a small business's profit.)*
- **Group.** A partner raises an objection as an open question because she was taught questions are gentler. It is heard as an open question. The person who knows the fatal fact answers only when asked directly. A lease is signed **because nobody objected, which is not the same as anybody agreeing.** Every fact needed to prevent the loss was already in the room.
- **Society.** Tenerife, 1977: a first officer voices a doubt about takeoff clearance in the softest available form to the airline's most senior captain. 583 dead. Challenger, 1986. Crew resource management exists because aviation fixed a shared process for how doubt is raised and how it must be answered.

**The narrow claim:** a shared process doesn't make people agree, and it isn't about being nice. It separates *how something is said* from *whether it is true*, so the second can be checked. Where that separation is missing, groups get *less* conflict, not more, and become less correct, because disagreement goes underground.

## The companion piece: "why I offered it to you"

The same conversation produced the shortest version of the pitch, written for someone who read the principle and declined it: *"Right now you have a number in your head for how well you understood that sentence. I don't. That's the whole problem."* Then it runs on itself: *"I just claimed verification matters. Do you know I meant what you received? Give me a number."* The draft can close on that.

## Why article-worthy

It reframes feedback-culture advice (be direct / be kind) as a coordination problem where the specific rule matters less than everyone running the same one. That is a claim a reader can test in their own team this week.
