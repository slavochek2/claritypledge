# "Do You Want to Win?" Is the Useless Question

> Working title, `[FOUNDER DECISION: title]`. Alternatives: "Two Goals Nobody Named" · "Right About the Product, Wrong About the Room."

## Arc

**ARC-5 (The Encounter That Broke It).** The author of a verify-before-you-build method joins a weekend hackathon with strangers, disagrees often and calmly, and turns out mostly right about the product. He still leaves drained, with a team that bonded without him. The method was never run in that room. The break shows a missing layer: before arguing about the product, verify what each person is actually there for, and who decides what.

## Idea

A team of strangers, about 30 hours, everyone saying they want to win. The founder built a live app overnight, on an idea he wasn't keen on, and argued for a live product over a mock demo, for recording over typing, and for the name already on the domain he had reserved. Each objection the team raised ("we want to learn and improve it," "it's just a demo," "you're making it difficult") sounded irrational to him.

The reread: the objections were rational answers to a different goal. Some teammates were there to learn and enjoy the weekend, and he was there to win with something real. **Two incompatible goals, never stated, make each side's arguments sound irrelevant to the other.** That isn't irrationality versus reason. It's a gap in understanding that came before every disagreement they had.

**Why the obvious check failed:** he did ask "we want to win, right?" Everyone said yes. A closed question that costs nothing to agree with reveals nothing. What would have surfaced the gap is a question that forces a trade-off: *"would you rather win with a product you barely touched, or lose with one you learned a lot building?"* · *"are you fine with agents writing most of this?"* · *"what would make this weekend worth it even if we don't place?"*

**The author's own move, named:** reserving the domain before the name was agreed, and repeating "we will win if…" as settled fact, read as decisions made *for* the group rather than *with* it. Being right about the strategy didn't make those moves verified.

**The second finding (bonding asymmetry):** the team spent its hours together discussing, and the builder spent his alone building. Shared time bonds and solitary work, however valuable, does not. A steady dissenter can become the thing a group bonds around.

## Why article-worthy

Every team, not just hackathon teams, asks the closed version ("are we aligned?", "we all want this to succeed?") and gets unanimous agreement that means nothing. The piece gives a portable replacement (trade-off questions plus decision rights agreed upfront) and shows it through the author's own failure in a room where his method would have applied. It also bears on the hackathon vehicle discussed 2026-10-06: hackathons run on time pressure, and this is a dated, first-person case of what skipping the check costs.
