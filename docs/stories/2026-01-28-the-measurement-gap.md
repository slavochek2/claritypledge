# The Day I Discovered No One Measures Understanding

**Draft for:** Newsletter / founder journey content
**Date:** 2026-01-28
**Status:** Draft

---

## The Block

I was stuck.

I'd built a tool that works. When two people use /live to verify understanding, the gap closes. They walk away actually aligned, not just assuming alignment. I'd seen it happen dozens of times.

But I couldn't figure out how to make money.

The obvious path — run workshops, hope people use the free tool, hope they bring it to work, hope businesses pay — had too many "hopes." Each step required someone to subjectively evaluate whether this was worth their time or money. And I have a deep discomfort with that kind of selling. It feels like asking people to evaluate *me*, not a product.

I was paralyzed.

---

## The First Insight

Then I realized something: **the tool reveals a blindspot people don't know they have.**

Think about it. If you're miscalibrated — you think you understood someone but you didn't — you have no way of knowing. You walk away confident. The gap is invisible to you.

So the person who's blind won't pay to fix their blindness. They don't know they're blind.

But someone else might see it. A coach working with a client. A manager watching their team. They see the miscalibration that the person themselves can't see.

**The person who SEES the blindspot will pay.**

That pointed me toward coaches. Executive coaches, leadership coaches, communication coaches. They see their clients' listening gaps every day. They just can't prove it.

---

## The Deeper Question

But then I asked a harder question: **Does any of this actually matter?**

I'd been assuming that calibration — verified understanding — leads to better outcomes. Better sales. Better retention. Better collaboration. But what if that's just a nice story? What if I'm building on sand?

I decided to find out before spending more time.

---

## The Research

I ran a systematic investigation:

**Phase 0: What do other fields call this?**

Turns out "listener calibration" has many names:
- *Listening fidelity* (communication studies)
- *Comprehension monitoring* (education)
- *Grounding* (linguistics)
- *Active listening* (psychology)
- *Teach-back* (healthcare)

The concept is everywhere. The terminology is fragmented.

**Track A: Does it actually matter?**

I found a 2023 meta-analysis. 400,000 observations. The correlation between perceived listening and work outcomes: **r=.39**. That's a medium-to-large effect in applied psychology.

Strongest links:
- Sales performance: r=.47
- Reduced burnout: r=.28

And in healthcare, a practice called "teach-back" — where doctors ask patients to explain back their treatment instructions — **reduces hospital readmissions by 60%**. That's experimental evidence. Randomized controlled trials. Verification loops work.

The thesis is solid.

**Track B: What's the competition?**

This is where it got interesting.

I looked at every tool I could find:
- **Gong, Chorus:** Measure talk-to-listen *ratio*. How much time you talked vs listened. Not whether you understood.
- **360 feedback:** Listening is one checkbox among 30 leadership competencies. Rated once a year. Generic.
- **Self-assessment:** People rate their own listening. But overestimating your listening is the whole problem.
- **Academic scales:** AELS, ALOS, HURIER exist in research papers. No one productized them.

I kept looking for someone measuring whether the listener actually understood what the speaker meant.

I couldn't find anyone.

---

## The Gap

Here's what I realized:

> **Everyone believes listening matters. No one measures whether understanding actually happened.**

The research proves listening correlates with outcomes. Companies spend billions on communication training. Gong raised $500M+ to analyze sales calls.

But no one — *no one* — measures the thing that actually matters: did the listener understand what the speaker meant?

They measure talk time. They measure engagement. They measure self-reported listening ability. They measure everything except understanding.

---

## The Moat

This changed how I think about the product.

I'm not competing with Gong. Gong measures how much you talked. I measure whether you understood.

I'm not competing with 360 feedback. That's one checkbox among 30, rated by people guessing.

I'm not competing with training. Training has a 12% transfer rate. People learn skills and don't apply them.

I'm **productizing listening fidelity** — a concept academia validated decades ago but no one built into a product.

The explain-back protocol isn't new. Hospitals use it. Coaches teach it. What's new is measuring it systematically over time.

---

## Protocol-Led Growth

One more insight emerged.

The explain-back protocol can spread without my product. Coaches can teach it to clients. Clients can teach it to teams. It's simple and valuable.

But if you want to:
- Track your calibration over time
- Prove improvement to a manager or coach
- Create a trust signal that others can see

...you need measurement.

**The protocol spreads free. The measurement captures value.**

I'm calling this "Protocol-Led Growth." The protocol is the distribution channel. The product is the proof layer.

---

## Where I Am Now

I started the day paralyzed by uncertainty.

I end the day with:
- Validated thesis (r=.39, teach-back works)
- Clear market gap (no one measures understanding)
- Defined competitive position (not Gong, not 360, not training)
- A growth model (protocol spreads free, measurement is product)
- A validation plan (event with coaches, not 1-on-1 sales calls)

The uncertainty isn't gone. I still don't know if coaches will pay. But I know the foundation is solid.

And I know something the market doesn't seem to know yet:

**Everyone believes listening matters. No one measures it. I do.**
